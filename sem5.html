Island context

Why the lagoon

During the group analysis phase, population projections were mapped against existing land capacity using UN development guidelines. The findings were straightforward: at current growth rates, Gaafaru’s residential plots will be exhausted before 2050. The lagoon was not chosen as a site of opportunity — it was identified as the only available direction for expansion. Climatic forces, ecological systems, and bathymetric conditions were then overlaid to determine where within the lagoon a settlement could responsibly sit. The western pocket did not offer opportunity. It offered constraint — and that is where this project begins.

Chapter I

Searching — letting the imagination run

Sketches & Studies

These came after the field trip. Something about being physically on the water shifted the thinking — the question stopped being “what should this look like” and became “how would people actually live here in 2050.” How would they earn. How would they move. What would feel normal after a generation. The sketches that came out of that period were looser and more varied than anything before — different media, different scales, different programmes. That variety was not indecision. It was the project refusing to close too early.

The Collage

The brief was to map the problem of the island through image. The idea was clear enough: before an island is an island, something arrives. Usually a coconut — carried by current, landing on a sandbank, beginning a process that takes centuries. What happened instead was that the coconut took over. It became too literal, too dominant, and the collage stopped being an argument about settlement and became an image about a seed.

What I understand now is that the coconut was never wrong as an idea. It was just not yet translated. The settlement that eventually emerged still carries it — a hull that arrives, anchors, and slowly becomes part of the place it landed in.

Concept Visions

The pin-up was incomplete. Some of what mattered most that semester never made it to the board — the social questions, how a floating settlement avoids becoming a separate community from the island it sits beside, who controls it, how it is governed, whether the people who need it most can actually access it. What the pin-up exposed was the gap between thinking and communicating. The ideas were further along than the boards suggested. That gap is what Chapter II had to close.

Journal & Touchstone

The sticky notes came before the drawings. Programme, anchoring logic, the two-core structure, the word cultivate — these were the questions the journal was holding while the design was still too uncertain to commit to paper. Small format forces economy. You only write what you actually think.

The touchstone model started here — a pencil sketch working out what anchoring could look like before the foam and cardboard made it physical.

Chapter II

Testing — making to find out

Sketch Masterplan & Touchstone

Designing from the unit up is instinctive — you know the room, then the floor, then the building. At 150 units the brain stops working that way. The only way through was abstraction: place something, react to it, place something else, until what started as marks on paper became a settlement with logic.

The touchstone was an exercise in letting go of authorship. The forms were not designed — they were the result of forces applied to material: pressure, fold, resistance. Not what should this look like. What would this become if the forces decided.

Cluster Models

The cluster read as bulky. Units too close, too uniform, the shared space between them unclear. Modelling it at this scale made that visible in a way drawing hadn’t — the settlement felt dense rather than inhabited.

The later cluster is the same idea resolved: circular hulls finding their spacing, pathways emerging between them rather than being imposed. The relationship between units is legible. A neighbourhood is starting to exist.

Dwelling Development

The curved form made this model structurally difficult to hold together. Every joint was fighting the geometry. That fragility was information: if the model couldn’t hold the curves, the design needed to earn them structurally before it could claim them formally.

It arrived damaged from transport and the hull shows it. But despite that — this model communicated the form and some spatial sequence more clearly than the large drawings on the board did.

Structure & Detail

Several people asked if this was a conceptual model. It isn’t — it is the structure. The radial beams and ring geometry produce a rhythm that most structural models don’t have, because most structures are designed first and formed around afterward. Here the form came first and the structure had to find its way through it. The building moves because the bones move.

If the structure yields to the form, and the form yields to the water, then even the smallest fixing has to carry that logic.

Process Timeline

This table is the two weeks of non-stop work laid flat. Every model in sequence, every drawing taped out below it. Seeing it this way — everything made, everything that failed, everything that almost worked — was the first time the process looked like a process rather than a series of separate attempts.

What the Making Taught

IOn the model submission

The pin-up hadn’t gone well. I put more into the model submission thinking that would turn things around. It didn’t. If you don’t know which one is failing you — the making or the concept — you don’t know what to fix. Both needed to change. They had to be worked simultaneously, not in sequence.

IIOn what needed to change

The final submission required a different approach entirely. Less building to see what happens, more deciding what the project actually was and then building to prove it. The form, the cluster logic, the section — these needed to be settled before the model started, not discovered through it.

IIIOn getting there

The water kept pushing back. At some point I stopped treating that as a problem to be solved and started treating it as the design. That shift — from resisting the constraint to working inside it — is what yield actually means. Not as a concept. As a practice.

Chapter III

Yielding — the proposal

Yield, Register, Transmit

Three conditions, not three steps. The settlement yields to force — wave direction, tide, wind — rather than resisting it. It registers the environment — light, humidity, current — as lived experience rather than filtered noise. It transmits what it learns to the people inside it.

The Problem

At this point the dwelling had an outer organic shell — marine concrete, metallic, protective — and nothing else. It felt like armour, closer to a bunker than a home. The suggestion came simply: put something living through the centre of it. Vegetation running vertically through the core — not as decoration, but as the counterweight to the hardness of the shell. Soft against hard. Growing against fixed.

That one addition completed the argument — a building that registers its environment from the outside and transmits it, filtered and alive, to the inside.

Masterplan

The settlement is organised around two cores. The northern core holds heritage and administrative programmes — the civic weight of the community. The southern core serves tourism and retreat — quieter, more permeable to the lagoon edge. Radial pathways extend from each core to the perimeter, keeping every unit within walking distance of shared facilities and the island ferry terminal, while commercial programme at the ends of each arm distributes economic activity to the edges.

The ferry connection is not an afterthought; it is the spine the whole settlement orients around.

Cluster

Each cluster groups six units around a shared pathway node. Six clusters form a neighbourhood. The pathways are doubled — parallel routes so that if one is blocked or damaged, movement continues. The space between routes is planted with mangrove, fitted with benches, fishing platforms, shaded decks. The redundancy that was designed for resilience becomes, in practice, the social infrastructure of the settlement.

Floor Plans & Elevation

The ground floor is not storage. It is the household economy — workshop, small trade, aquaculture staging, craft production.

Kitchen, dining and living arranged around the central core without fixed division. The louvres do not block the lagoon; they pace it.

Three private rooms cantilever outward toward the lagoon. The space between them is where the family actually gathers.

An organic metallic exoskeleton rising from waterline to roof — the one that protects — around the one that grows.

Section

The section is where the settlement stops performing and starts breathing. Read vertically, the dwelling is a single continuous movement: lagoon surface, hull void, living platform, open roof — each layer yielding to what comes from above or below. Ventilation is not designed in; it is the consequence of not blocking it.

The floor plane, set 400mm above the hull deck, creates the threshold: below is the lagoon’s logic, above is the dwelling’s. The structure does not filter the climate — it registers it.

Exploded Assembly

The unit is not built by one contractor. It is distributed across the trades that already exist within the island economy — marine concrete specialists, local timber workers, prefab composite fabricators, carpentry cooperatives, PV installers. A single unit reaches completion in approximately twelve weeks. Across 150+ lagoon units, that parallelism becomes an economic strategy.

Details: below the waterline

The detail below the waterline is where yield becomes engineering. The anchoring wire carries oyster attachment points — the structure doesn’t just sit in the lagoon, it begins to become part of it over time. The coral cage at the base is the same logic: not mitigation, but integration. The hull protects above; below, it invites.

  • Deck bollard, teak decking, access hatch, sub-deck structure
  • Hull ring beam, marine fender, reinforced marine concrete, water stopper
  • Marine systems pump-out, anchoring wire with oyster, coral cage, ballast

Details: at the top

At the top of the building the same principle applies in reverse. The tensile greening mesh holds planting against the FRP roof panel. The exterior light shelf bounces afternoon light deeper into the interior without heat. The PV array above powers the unit. Nothing here is added on — each element is doing two things at once, which is the only way a building this exposed to salt, sun, and water can afford to be detailed.

Atmosphere

The balcony overlooks the settlement. There is no railing high enough to hide the horizon. The settlement does not frame the ocean as a view — it places you inside it.

Vertical planting runs along the core not as decoration but as a daily register of the environment. Moving through the building means moving through something alive.

“The gaps between them are not leftover space — they are channels that allow water, wind, and light to move through the cluster the way current moves through reef structure. The settlement does not interrupt the lagoon’s logic. It joins it.”

Introspection

Looking back

IOn communication

The collage submission failed twice. Not because the idea was wrong, but because I was using different media for different parts without asking whether they spoke the same language. A collage is not a collection of things you find interesting. It is an argument made through images. If I were to make it again, every element would be chosen because it could not be replaced by any other.

IIOn process visibility

The Part 2 feedback noted that the design appeared to jump straight to a final form without showing how it developed. The models existed, but they were not arranged to tell that story. A process is only evidence if it is made legible. Documentation is not separate from design. It is part of how design justifies itself to others, and eventually to yourself.

IIIOn yielding

Yield, register, transmit came from the accumulated pressure of iteration: forms too rigid, structures that imposed too much on the seabed, a settlement logic that tried to control rather than respond. The water kept pushing back. That shift is what I want to carry forward — not as a method, but as a disposition.
